# Sources, and Where to Check Me

Every substantive claim in this book, with what it rests on and how confident you should be. If a claim isn't in this list, treat it as my judgement rather than established fact.

## Strong evidence — act on these

**Protein breakpoint ≈ 1.6 g/kg/day, range to 2.2.** Morton et al. 2018, a meta-analysis of 49 studies and 1,863 participants, found fat-free-mass gains from resistance training stopped increasing above about 1.62 g/kg/day, with a confidence interval running roughly 1.03–2.20. Later, larger analyses have broadly confirmed the ceiling. *Confidence: high for the population; your individual optimum sits somewhere in that range and can't be pinned down further.*

**Sleep restriction shifts weight loss from fat to lean mass.** Nedeltcheva et al. 2010, *Annals of Internal Medicine*. Ten adults, controlled clinical setting, identical calorie restriction, 5.5 vs 8.5 hours sleep for 14 days. Sleep restriction reduced the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55% and increased fat-free mass loss by 60%. *Confidence: high on the mechanism and direction; the sample was small, so treat the exact percentages as indicative.*

**Training 1–3 reps short of failure produces essentially the same hypertrophy as training to failure.** Refalo, Helms, Trexler, Hamilton and Fyfe 2023, *Sports Medicine* — a meta-analysis pooling 15 studies. Closer-to-failure training was favoured but the effect size was small (0.15–0.21). A 2024 within-subject trial in trained lifters found similar hypertrophy from failure and from reps-in-reserve training. *Confidence: high.*

**10–20 hard sets per muscle per week is the productive range.** Consistent across the hypertrophy literature and the basis of Helms's volume guidance. *Confidence: high for the range; low for any specific number within it, which is why Part 6 makes it Experiment 2.*

**Gaining at 0.24–0.38% of bodyweight per week yields roughly 70–75% of the gain as lean mass.** From the bulking-rate literature; Helms recommends advanced lifters cap at 0.25%/week. *Confidence: moderate-to-high on the direction, moderate on the exact ratios.*

**Body recomposition works reliably in untrained beginners, people with higher body fat, and returning lifters.** Well documented across the resistance training literature; a 2020 *Strength and Conditioning Journal* review covers the trained-lifter case specifically. *Confidence: high for the three groups named, lower for lean trained lifters.*

**Roughly half of people starting an exercise program quit within six months, with the sharpest drop between weeks six and twelve.** Adherence literature, including a one-year follow-up of novice exercisers in fitness-club settings. *Confidence: high.*

## Moderate evidence — reasonable to plan around

**Lyle McDonald's muscle-gain model** — ~9 kg year one, halving annually. A widely used rule of thumb, not a controlled finding. Most people land well below it. *Confidence: moderate as an upper bound, low as a prediction.*

**Casey Butt's maximum-muscular-potential formula.** Derived from measurements of drug-tested top-level natural bodybuilders. More conservative than competing models, which is a point in its favour. A 1 cm wrist difference moves the prediction by 2–3 kg, so measurement error matters. *Confidence: moderate; it's a well-constructed estimate from a real dataset, not a law.*

**Navy tape body-fat formula.** Accurate to roughly ±3–4 percentage points; degrades at the extremes and on very muscular people. *Confidence: adequate for tracking change, poor for absolute accuracy — which is all you need it for.*

**Mifflin-St Jeor TDEE estimation.** Best-validated of the common equations, error bar around ±300 kcal for an individual, mostly from unmeasured NEAT variation. *Confidence: fine as a starting guess, not as an answer.*

**Concurrent training interference is real but modest**, and depends on modality, proximity and volume. *Confidence: moderate.*

## Weak or contested — I've flagged these in text

**Somatotypes as a training or diet framework.** No evidence links somatotype to which protocol works better. Sheldon's original theory was ideologically loaded and methodologically poor, and the personality half was abandoned by the 1950s. Somatotype scores survive as descriptive tools in sports science — recent work still uses them to characterise athlete populations across sports — which is a different and much weaker claim than the one the fitness industry makes. *Confidence that it's not actionable: high.*

**Whether the true optimum protein intake is above 1.6 g/kg for lean hard-training people.** An active argument; some analyses support higher intakes. The practical difference is small. *Unresolved.*

**Frequency effects when volume is equated.** Studies point in different directions. Probably individual, which is why it's Experiment 3.

## Where to check the research yourself

- **Stronger By Science** (strongerbyscience.com) — Greg Nuckols and Eric Trexler. Free, rigorous, and unusually good at explaining what studies *don't* show.
- **Examine.com** — supplement and nutrition claims, graded by evidence quality.
- **MASS Research Review** — Helms, Nuckols, Trexler and colleagues. Paid, monthly, current.
- **The r/Fitness wiki** (thefitness.wiki) — free, practical, the routines and the basics.
- **PubMed** — for reading the actual papers rather than someone's summary of them, including mine.

## What I'd want you to distrust in this book

Three things, honestly.

**All the specific numbers.** Every threshold, cutoff and target here is a population statistic applied to one person. The frame-classification cutoffs, the body-fat brackets, the calorie multipliers — all of them have real error bars that a clean table hides. Tables always look more certain than the data behind them.

**The program.** It's a reasonable default assembled from well-tested principles. It is not tested as a unit and it isn't optimal for you, because nothing is until you've run the loop in Part 6.

**The anecdote chapter.** Recurring themes across thousands of self-reports are suggestive and they are not evidence. I flagged that at the top of it and I'll flag it again here.

The correct relationship to this book is the one it argues for throughout: use it as a starting position, then let your own logbook overrule it. When the log and the book disagree about your body, the log is right.
