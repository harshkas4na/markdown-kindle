# The Mistakes That Cost Years

Ranked by how much time they waste. Every one of these is common, and most of them feel like diligence from the inside — which is exactly why they persist.

## 1. Quitting in weeks six to ten

The largest cost by far, because it costs everything. Roughly half of people who start a program have stopped within six months, and the sharpest drop-off is right in that window: the initial motivation has decayed, the rapid novice strength gains have slowed, and nothing visible has happened yet.

The trap is that the internal experience is *accurate*. You genuinely haven't seen visible change. The conclusion — "this isn't working" — is the wrong inference from a correct observation, because visible change was never scheduled to arrive yet.

**The fix:** know the timeline before you start, measure the log rather than the mirror, and lower the bar on bad weeks instead of missing. Attendance is the only variable that matters in this window.

## 2. Never progressively overloading

The person who does the same three sets of ten with the same weight every week for a year. They trained fifty-two times. They provided zero new stimulus. They will look identical, and they will reasonably conclude that lifting doesn't work for them.

**The fix:** write down what you lift and beat it. That's the whole intervention, and it's free.

## 3. Program-hopping

Six weeks on a program, no visible result, switch. Repeat. A year later there's no lift with twelve continuous weeks of data on it.

Every recommended program works. They differ by maybe 10–15%. Switching costs you the entire test.

**The fix:** twelve weeks minimum, no exceptions, and judge by the log.

## 4. Under-eating protein while trying to build muscle

Very common in people otherwise doing everything right, and especially common on Indian vegetarian diets where the default plate lands around 50–60 g a day against a target of 110–150.

**The fix:** count it for two weeks. If you're short, add one deliberate protein source per meal.

## 5. Bulking dirty

Gaining a kilo a week when the maximum possible muscle gain is under 0.2 kg a week. Five-sixths of that is fat, and it all has to come off later.

**The fix:** the rate rules in Part 4. +0.25 to +0.5% of bodyweight per week, checked against the weekly average, adjusted in 200 kcal steps.

## 6. Cutting badly

The full combination: aggressive deficit, protein cut along with everything else, weights dropped in favour of high-rep "toning" and cardio. The reliable outcome is a smaller version of the same body — the exact thing the person was trying to avoid.

**The fix:** deficit under 25%, protein at the top of the range, keep the working weights.

## 7. Doing far too much volume

Six days a week, twenty-five sets a muscle, everything to failure, from month one. The most-cited programming error among natural lifters, and it doesn't feel like a mistake — it feels like commitment. The cost shows up as stalled progress, aching joints and burnout around month three.

**The fix:** start at the bottom of the 10–20 set range. Add volume only when you've plateaued and everything else checks out.

## 8. Ignoring sleep

Two years of optimising training and nutrition while sleeping five and a half hours. The Nedeltcheva data says the same diet with short sleep loses less than half the fat and substantially more muscle. It is a first-tier variable being treated as a lifestyle footnote.

**The fix:** ninety minutes earlier. It costs less than the supplements.

## 9. Never measuring anything

No log, no photos, no tape, no scale trend. Then, in month eight, an argument with yourself about whether anything is happening — an argument neither side can win, because there's no data.

**The fix:** the four-minutes-a-week system in Part 6.

## 10. Oscillating between bulk and cut

Three weeks bulking, feel soft, three weeks cutting, feel small, repeat. A year of genuine effort at exactly the same bodyweight and body fat.

**The fix:** pick one, write the end date, don't re-evaluate before it.

## 11. Training through sharp pain

Distinguishing muscular difficulty from joint warning. Working through the second kind is the most common route to a problem that costs six months, and a meaningful fraction of people who get injured never resume at all.

**The fix:** sharp, localised, repeatable pain stops the set. Immediately. Not at the end of the set.

## 12. Ego lifting

Weight selected to impress rather than to hit the rep target at the right effort. Costs form, costs the progression, and is the leading cause of the injuries in item 11.

**The fix:** the weight is decided by the rep target, not by what's on the bar next to you.

## 13. Believing you're a special case

"My body doesn't respond." "I'm an ectomorph." "My metabolism is broken."

The overwhelming majority of the time, one of the first eight items on this list is true instead, and the label is functioning as an explanation for it. Genuine non-response to resistance training and adequate nutrition is rare enough that it should be the last hypothesis considered, not the first.

**The fix:** work down the diagnostic in Part 3, chapter 5 honestly before concluding anything about your genetics.

## 14. Optimising the tiny things while ignoring the big ones

Reading about protein timing while missing your protein total. Debating rest-period length while never adding weight. Buying a fourth supplement while sleeping six hours.

The hierarchy in Part 1 exists specifically to prevent this, and it's still the easiest mistake to make, because the small things are interesting and the big things are boring and already known.

**The fix:** before changing anything, walk the hierarchy from the top and stop at the first row you're not actually doing.

## The pattern underneath all of them

Twelve of these fourteen come down to the same two failures: **not measuring**, and **not waiting long enough**.

Measurement is four minutes a week. Waiting is free. Between them they'd prevent almost every wasted year described in this chapter — which is a strange thing to be true, and it's true anyway.
