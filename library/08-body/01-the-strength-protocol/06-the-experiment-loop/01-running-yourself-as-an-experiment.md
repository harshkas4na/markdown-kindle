# Running Yourself as an Experiment

Everything before this part was priors. This is where you start generating your own data, because the research knows about people in general and you need to know about you.

## Why an n=1 experiment is worth anything at all

A study with a thousand participants tells you what the average response is and how widely responses vary around it. It does not tell you where in that distribution you sit — and in exercise science the distributions are *wide*. Studies of identical training protocols routinely find some participants gaining substantially and others gaining almost nothing, on the same program.

You are one draw from that distribution. The literature narrows your search space from "anything" to "somewhere in this range." Your own experiments tell you where in the range you are.

This is the reason a good coach beats a good program: not because they know a secret, but because they're running this loop on you continuously.

## The rules of self-experimentation

**One variable at a time.** This is the whole discipline. If you add volume, raise protein and start sleeping more in the same block, you learn nothing about which of the three did it. The temptation to change everything is enormous, especially when you're motivated. Resist it — a year of one-variable blocks teaches you your body; a year of everything-at-once teaches you nothing.

**Twelve weeks minimum per block.** Shorter and the measurement noise swamps the effect. Bodyweight fluctuates 1–2 kg daily; strength varies session to session by more than a month's worth of real progress.

**Define the outcome measure before you start.** Write down what "worked" means, in numbers, on day one. Otherwise you'll decide afterward, and you will decide in whichever direction protects the choice you already made.

**Keep everything else fixed.** Especially the things you don't think matter. Change the program *and* your gym *and* your job schedule and the result is uninterpretable.

**Write it down.** A block you didn't record didn't happen. In six months you will not remember what your sets were in March.

## The experiment card

One per block. Fill in the top four fields before you begin, the bottom three at the end.

```
BLOCK #___          Start: ______      End: ______ (12+ weeks)

Hypothesis:     e.g. "16 sets/week for chest will grow it
                faster than the 10 I've been doing."

Variable changed:  ONE thing. Name it precisely.

Held constant:     Program, calories, protein, sleep target,
                   training days, gym.

Outcome measure:   The number(s) that decide it, and the
                   threshold that counts as success.
                   e.g. "chest measurement +1.5 cm AND
                   bench 3x8 weight up 5 kg."

--- at the end ---

Result:            The actual numbers.

Confounders:       What else changed that shouldn't have?
                   Be honest. There's always something.

Verdict:           Keep / revert / test further.
```

## The experiment queue

Run them in roughly this order. The early ones have the largest effects and are the cheapest to test.

**Block 1 — Baseline.** Change nothing. Run the default program and your Pass 2 instruction exactly as written for twelve weeks, and record everything. You cannot measure a change without a baseline, and almost everyone skips this step and then wonders why nothing is interpretable.

**Block 2 — Volume.** The single most individual variable in training. Add four to six sets per week to your worst-responding muscle group, hold everything else. Did it grow faster, or did it just get more tiring? This finds your position in the 10–20 sets range.

**Block 3 — Frequency.** Same total weekly volume, split across more sessions. Chest twice a week instead of once, same sets. Some people respond noticeably; some don't notice at all.

**Block 4 — Rep range.** If you've lived at 5s, spend twelve weeks at 8–12. If you've lived at 12s, go to 5s. The literature says both work when effort is matched; your logbook says which one *you* progress on more reliably.

**Block 5 — Calorie rate.** If your bulk added more waist than you wanted, run the next one 150 kcal lower and compare the muscle-to-waist ratio over the same duration.

**Block 6 — Exercise selection.** Swap one lift for a variation in the same pattern. Incline press for flat, front squat for back. Track both the loading and the measurement.

## What you're building

After two years of this you'll have eight or nine filled-in cards, and together they say something no book can: *this is how my body responds.* You'll know your volume tolerance as a number. You'll know whether frequency matters for you. You'll know what surplus rate keeps your waist flat.

That document is your actual body type. It took two years and a logbook to write, it's specific to you, and it's worth more than every chart in Part 2 — including the ones I wrote.

## The failure modes

**Changing the variable mid-block** because week five felt slow. Week five always feels slow.

**Judging by the mirror.** You see yourself daily and cannot detect gradual change. Use the tape, the log and the photos.

**Not controlling the confounders you don't want to admit to.** The block where you also started sleeping better, or the block that spanned a holiday. Note them on the card. A block with a known confounder isn't worthless — it just doesn't get to answer the question you asked.

**Testing something too small to detect.** Creatine timing, protein distribution, rest-period length within a normal range — these have effects far smaller than your measurement noise. You cannot resolve a 1% effect with a ±2% instrument. Test big things.
