# Progressive Overload and How Hard to Go

Two questions, and they're the two that decide whether the program in the last chapter does anything: *when does the weight go up*, and *how close to failure should each set be*.

## The progression rule

Use **double progression**. It's the simplest rule that works and it's the one I'd hand anyone who hasn't got a specific reason to use something fancier.

Each exercise has a rep range — say 3 sets of 8–12.

1. Pick a weight you can do for 8 reps on all three sets.
2. Each session, try to add reps. 8/8/8 becomes 9/8/8, then 10/9/8, and so on.
3. When you hit the **top** of the range on **all** sets — 12/12/12 — add weight next session.
4. The reps will drop back to the bottom of the range. Start climbing again.

How much weight to add:

| Lift | Increment |
|---|---|
| Squat, deadlift | 5 kg (2.5 kg once progress slows) |
| Bench, row | 2.5 kg |
| Overhead press | 2.5 kg, then 1.25 kg — buy micro-plates, they're worth it |
| Dumbbell work | Next dumbbell up, which is often a large jump — take the rep drop |

For the 3×5 strength work in Tier 1, use straight linear progression instead: hit all reps, add weight next session, every session. That's the whole novice phase, and it works for maybe six to nine months before it stops.

## When linear progression stops working

It will. That's not failure, it's graduation. When you miss reps on the same lift three sessions running:

1. **Deload that lift 10%** and work back up. Sometimes that's all it needed.
2. If it stalls again at the same place, **switch that lift to double progression** in a wider rep range.
3. If it stalls a third time, you're an intermediate on that lift. Move to Tier 2 for it.

Different lifts stall at different times. Overhead press usually stalls first because the increments are large relative to the load; deadlift usually last. It's normal to be running linear progression on some lifts and double progression on others.

## How hard each set should be

This is where RIR comes in. **Reps in Reserve** is how many more reps you could have done if you'd kept going. RIR 2 means you stopped with two in the tank. It's the inverse of RPE on the 10-point scale — RIR 0 is RPE 10, RIR 1 is RPE 9, and so on.

**Train most working sets at RIR 1–3.**

The evidence: a 2023 meta-analysis by Refalo, Helms and colleagues pooled 15 studies comparing training at different distances from failure. Training closer to failure was favoured, but the effect size was small — somewhere in the 0.15–0.21 range. A more recent within-subject trial in trained lifters found the same hypertrophy from training to momentary failure as from stopping with reps in reserve.

The practical translation, and the sentence worth remembering: **going from 2 sets to 3 sets usually buys you more than going from 3 RIR to 0 RIR.** Effort is not free. Sets taken to true failure cost disproportionately more recovery, which limits the volume you can do next session, which is the variable that actually drove the result.

So: stop one to three reps short on almost everything. Save actual failure for the last set of isolation work, where the fatigue cost is low and the feedback about your RIR calibration is useful.

## You are probably bad at estimating RIR

Almost everyone is, and beginners are systematically bad in one direction: they stop far earlier than they think. A set the lifter calls "2 RIR" is often 5 or 6.

Two ways to calibrate:

- **Occasionally take a set to true failure** on a safe isolation exercise — a machine, a cable, a dumbbell curl. Count how many reps you got past the point where you'd normally have stopped. That gap is your calibration error.
- **Watch bar speed.** When the rep speed drops noticeably despite maximum intent, you're within about two reps of failure. This is more reliable than how it feels.

## The four ways to progress, ranked

Weight on the bar is the obvious one and it isn't always available. When it isn't, progress another way:

| Method | When to use it |
|---|---|
| **More weight** | Default. Always the first choice. |
| **More reps at the same weight** | The other half of double progression. |
| **More sets** | When you've plateaued on both of the above and recovery allows. Add slowly — one set per muscle per week. |
| **Better execution** | Deeper squat, paused bench, no bounce, controlled eccentric. Progress that doesn't show in the log but is real. |

What is *not* progression: doing the same thing faster, cutting rest, chasing a pump, feeling more tired afterward, or adding a new exercise. Fatigue is a cost, not a result.

## The mental model

The stimulus is a signal, not a punishment. You are trying to tell your body "this load is now a regular demand, adapt to it" — with the smallest dose that delivers the message, so that you have recovery left over to actually adapt. That's Tim Ferriss's minimum effective dose applied correctly, and it's the reason the answer to "am I doing enough?" is usually yes, and the answer to "why am I not progressing?" is usually food or sleep.
