# Plateaus and Deloads

A plateau is not a mystery. It has four possible causes and they can be checked in order in about ten minutes. What people do instead is change the program, which is almost never the answer and is usually the thing that resets the clock.

## First: is it actually a plateau?

Two things get mistaken for plateaus and neither is one.

**A bad week.** Sleep, stress, illness, a hard week at work. One session where the weights felt heavy is noise. Progress at the intermediate stage is measured monthly, not weekly, and the week-to-week variation is larger than the week-to-week trend.

**Progress you're not measuring.** If your bench weight hasn't moved but your reps have gone from 5 to 8, that is progress and your log says so. If your bodyweight is flat but your waist is down 3 cm, that is progress and your tape says so. Check the log before concluding anything. This is why the log exists.

A real plateau is: **no increase in weight or reps on a given lift, over four or more weeks, with consistent attendance.**

## The diagnostic, in order

Walk down this list. Stop at the first "no."

| # | Check | If no |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Have I trained this lift at least 90% of scheduled sessions in the last 4 weeks? | The problem is attendance, not programming. |
| 2 | Is my bodyweight moving in the direction my Pass 2 instruction says it should? | The problem is food. Fix calories before touching training. |
| 3 | Am I hitting my protein target on at least 5 days a week? | The problem is protein. |
| 4 | Am I sleeping 7+ hours on most nights? | The problem is sleep, and it's a bigger problem than you think — see Part 5. |
| 5 | Have I deloaded in the last 8–10 weeks? | Deload now. See below. |
| 6 | Am I actually training close enough to failure — 1–3 RIR, not 6? | Push harder before adding anything. |
| 7 | Am I within 10–20 hard sets per week for this muscle? | Adjust volume — toward 10 if above 20, toward 20 if below 10. |

Only if all seven are "yes" do you change the program. In practice, rows 2, 3 and 4 account for the overwhelming majority of stalls, and rows 2 and 4 alone probably account for half.

## The deload

A deload is a planned week of reduced training to let accumulated fatigue drain, so the fitness underneath becomes visible again. You are not losing anything. Muscle is not lost in a week; strength expression comes back higher than it left.

**When:** every 8–10 weeks as a matter of routine, or immediately when three or more of these are true — joints achy, sleep disturbed, motivation gone, weights that felt easy feel heavy, resting heart rate elevated, appetite off.

**How.** Pick one, they work about equally well:

| Style | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Load deload** | Same sets and reps, 50–60% of usual weight |
| **Volume deload** | Same weight, half the sets |
| **Full rest** | 5–7 days off entirely |

Full rest is under-used and is the right answer when the fatigue is life fatigue rather than training fatigue. Nobody has ever lost their physique to a week off.

The hardest part of a deload is psychological. It feels like quitting. Reframe it: **the deload is when the adaptation you paid for actually shows up.** Training is the stimulus; growth happens in the recovery, and if you never let the fatigue clear you never see what you built.

## When it really is the program

All seven checks pass, you've deloaded, and it's still stuck. Now change *one* thing, and give it eight weeks before judging.

Change them in this order:

1. **Add volume** — one to two sets per week for the stuck muscle. Cheapest change, most likely to work.
2. **Change the rep range** — if you've lived at 5s, spend a block at 8–12. If you've lived at 12s, spend a block at 5s. Different rep ranges produce similar hypertrophy when effort is matched, but a change in stimulus often restarts a stalled lift.
3. **Change the exercise, same pattern** — back squat to front squat, barbell bench to dumbbell. New leverages, new stimulus, and often the stall was a technique fault the variation exposes.
4. **Change frequency** — from once a week to twice for that muscle, same total volume split across the two.
5. **Change the whole program** — last resort, and only after twelve-plus weeks on the current one.

One variable at a time. Change three at once and you learn nothing about which one worked, which is the same mistake as never changing anything, just more expensive.

## The plateaus that aren't fixable

Two honest ones.

**Novice progression ending.** You cannot add 2.5 kg a session forever. When linear progression dies, it's dead — that's not a plateau, it's the end of a phase, and the fix is a different progression model, not more effort.

**Approaching your ceiling.** If you're four-plus years in and near the numbers Part 2's ceiling chapter predicted for you, progress genuinely slows to something like a kilo of muscle a year. At that point, the goal shifts from "get bigger" to "stay strong and keep training," and that's a fine goal. Most of the value was already banked in years one through three.
