# What a Program Must Do

Before the program itself, the criteria. If you understand these five, you can evaluate any routine you find anywhere — including the one in the next chapter — instead of trusting whoever posted it.

## 1. It must be progressive

There must be a written rule for when the weight goes up. Not "add weight when you feel ready." An actual rule: *when I hit the top of the rep range on all sets, next session I add 2.5 kg.*

This is the difference between a program and a list of exercises. A list of exercises does not produce adaptation. A rule for making the list harder over time does.

If a routine you're reading doesn't state its progression rule, it isn't a program. Most Instagram workouts are not programs.

## 2. It must cover every movement pattern

Six patterns. Miss one for long enough and you build an imbalance that eventually becomes an injury.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Squat** (knee-dominant) | Back squat, front squat, leg press, split squat |
| **Hinge** (hip-dominant) | Deadlift, Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, back extension |
| **Horizontal push** | Bench press, dumbbell press, push-up, dip |
| **Horizontal pull** | Barbell row, dumbbell row, cable row, chest-supported row |
| **Vertical push** | Overhead press, dumbbell shoulder press |
| **Vertical pull** | Pull-up, chin-up, lat pulldown |

Two rules of thumb that fix the two most common imbalances: **pull at least as much as you push**, and **do not skip the hinge**. The classic beginner physique — big chest, rounded shoulders, no back, no hamstrings — is exactly what you get from a program that's three-quarters push.

## 3. It must land in the right volume range

Volume is counted in **hard sets per muscle per week**. A hard set means taken to within a few reps of failure. Warm-ups don't count. Sets where you stopped because the timer went off don't count.

The defensible range from the hypertrophy literature is **10–20 hard sets per muscle per week**, with the caveat from the last part that where *you* fall in that range is individual and has to be found by experiment.

Below 10 you're maintaining more than building. Above 20 you're mostly generating fatigue you have to recover from, and for most people past a certain point it actively hurts progress — the most common serious error natural lifters make is not too little volume but too much, which drives up fatigue and cortisol beyond what the training stimulus justifies.

Start at the bottom of the range. Add, don't subtract. It's much easier to notice "this is working and I have room for more" than to unwind six months of accumulated over-reaching.

## 4. It must be recoverable given your actual life

Sleep, job stress, food, and training all draw from one pool. A program that would be optimal for a student sleeping nine hours is not optimal for someone sleeping six and working twelve-hour days — not because they're less committed, but because the recovery isn't there and unrecovered training is just damage.

The practical version: **choose the frequency you can hold on your worst normal week, not your best one.** Three days you never miss beats five days you hit twice a month.

## 5. It must be boring

This is the criterion nobody wants. A good program has you doing largely the same lifts for months, which is precisely what makes progression visible. If the exercises change every week, you cannot tell whether you're getting stronger — you have no baseline. Novelty feels like progress and is the enemy of measuring it.

"Muscle confusion" is not a physiological mechanism. It's a marketing term for programs that are entertaining to consume.

## The corollary: program-hopping

The single most common way people waste years is switching programs every four to six weeks, always to the newest thing, always just before the current one would have produced a measurable result.

Every program in the r/Fitness recommended list works. GZCLP works, 5/3/1 works, PPL works, the Basic Beginner Routine works, Starting Strength works. They differ at the margins and the margins are worth maybe 10–15% of your result. Adherence is worth all of it.

> **Run one program for a minimum of twelve weeks before judging it.** If you switch sooner, you have not tested it — you've only tested your patience, and you failed.

## The one legitimate reason to change

You've run it for twelve-plus weeks, your log shows you've stopped progressing on the main lifts, and a deload plus a food check didn't restart it. Then, and only then, change *one* variable. Part 3's last chapter covers what to change and in what order.
