# The Hierarchy of What Matters

Eric Helms built two books around a single diagram: a pyramid where the wide base is the thing that decides most of your result and the thin tip is the thing the internet argues about most. The inversion is almost perfect. The volume of discussion about a topic is close to inversely proportional to how much it matters.

Here is the hierarchy, ordered by how much of your outcome each layer controls. Everything else in this book is an expansion of one of these rows.

| Rank | Layer | What it decides | How much people talk about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Adherence** | Whether anything happens at all | Almost never |
| 2 | **Calorie balance** | Whether you gain or lose weight | Constantly, badly |
| 3 | **Protein intake** | Whether that weight is muscle or not | A lot, and roughly correctly |
| 4 | **Progressive overload** | Whether training produces adaptation | Rarely, in these words |
| 5 | **Volume and frequency** | How fast adaptation happens | Endlessly |
| 6 | **Exercise selection** | Marginal, plus joint health | Endlessly |
| 7 | **Rep ranges, tempo, order** | Small | Endlessly |
| 8 | **Meal timing, carb timing** | Very small | A lot |
| 9 | **Supplements** | Nearly zero, except two | Enormously |

## Layer 1 is not motivational filler

Roughly half of people who start an exercise program have quit within six months, and the sharpest cliff is between weeks six and twelve. That window is not where the programs fail. It is where the novelty runs out and nothing has replaced it yet.

Which means the highest-leverage decision in this entire book is not which split you run. It is: **can you do this specific thing on a bad week?** A four-day program you run three days a week for two years beats a six-day program you run for nine weeks. Choose the program you would still do on a Tuesday when work went badly.

Everything in Part 3 is designed around this. The default program is three days, not six, and the extra days are an *upgrade you earn* rather than a starting requirement — because the failure mode of starting at six is quitting at week nine, and the failure mode of starting at three is progressing slightly slower than you could have.

## Layers 2 and 3 are the only ones with hard numbers

Calorie balance decides direction. Protein decides composition. Everything else in nutrition is a rounding error by comparison, and Part 4 says so at length.

This is worth internalising early because it kills an enormous amount of noise. Whether you eat six meals or two, whether you have carbs at night, whether you drink your protein or chew it — these are layer 8 questions. They are real, they are just tiny, and they are where people spend the attention they should be spending on layers 1 through 4.

## Layer 4 is the one nobody names

Progressive overload means: over time, your muscles must be asked to do more than they did before. More weight, more reps, more sets, better control, less rest — something must increase.

The reason this ranks so high and gets discussed so little is that it's invisible without a log. A person who does the same three sets of ten with the same 20 kg dumbbells every Monday for a year has trained 52 times and progressively overloaded zero times. They will look identical at the end. They will also swear the gym doesn't work for them, and from inside their experience, that's a completely reasonable conclusion.

The fix is embarrassingly simple: **write down what you lift.** If you take one action from this book before finishing it, make it this.

## The two supplements

Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g a day, any time, forever. It's the most-studied sports supplement in existence, the effect is small but real, and it's cheap. And whey or any protein powder — which is not a supplement in the pharmacological sense at all, it's just protein that's convenient, and its only job is closing the gap when layer 3 is hard to hit with food.

That's the chapter. Caffeine if you like, vitamin D if you're deficient and a blood test says so. Everything else on the shelf at the supplement store is layer 9 and layer 9 is nearly zero.

## Using the hierarchy

When you're stuck and about to change something, walk down the list from the top and stop at the first row you are not actually doing well.

Not progressing? Before you change your split, ask: have I trained consistently for eight weeks (1)? Is my weight moving in the direction I chose (2)? Am I hitting protein most days (3)? Is the weight or reps on my main lifts higher than it was two months ago (4)?

In practice the answer is almost always in the first four rows, and the change people reach for is almost always in the last four.
