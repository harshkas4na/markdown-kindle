# Protein

The one macronutrient with a real number attached. If you get calories and protein right and improvise everything else, you will do fine. If you get everything else right and miss protein, you'll gain more fat on a bulk and lose more muscle on a cut, and you'll blame the program.

## The number

**1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day.**

Where it comes from: a 2018 meta-analysis by Morton and colleagues, pooling 49 studies and 1,863 participants, found that gains in fat-free mass from resistance training stopped increasing above roughly **1.62 g/kg/day**. The confidence interval on that breakpoint ran from about 1.03 to 2.20 — which is why the commonly quoted range has 2.2 as its top end. It's not the optimum, it's the upper bound of the uncertainty around the optimum.

Later and larger analyses have broadly confirmed the same ceiling. Some researchers argue the true individual optimum sits higher than 1.6 for lean, hard-training people, and the argument isn't unreasonable — but the practical difference between 1.6 and 2.2 is small enough that it isn't worth agonising over. Pick a number in the range and hit it.

**Practical targets:**

| Bodyweight | Lower target (1.6 g/kg) | Upper target (2.2 g/kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 55 kg | 88 g | 121 g |
| 60 kg | 96 g | 132 g |
| 65 kg | 104 g | 143 g |
| 70 kg | 112 g | 154 g |
| 75 kg | 120 g | 165 g |
| 80 kg | 128 g | 176 g |
| 90 kg | 144 g | 198 g |

**Two adjustments.** If you're carrying a lot of fat, calculate from your *target* bodyweight rather than your current one — muscle needs the protein, adipose tissue doesn't. If you're in an aggressive deficit, sit at the top of the range rather than the bottom; protein's muscle-sparing effect matters most when energy is scarce.

## Distribution

Spread it across **three to five meals**, aiming for **25–40 g per meal**. The mechanism is that muscle protein synthesis responds to a per-meal dose and there's diminishing return above roughly 40 g in one sitting.

How much this actually matters: less than the internet suggests. Total daily intake is the dominant variable by a wide margin. If your life means two large meals, hit the total and don't worry. But if it's free to spread it out, spread it out.

The one meaningful timing point is a protein-containing meal **within a few hours either side of training** — and "a few hours" is deliberately loose, because the 30-minute anabolic window is not a real thing. If you trained fasted, eat afterward reasonably promptly. Otherwise it's not a decision worth attention.

## On protein powder

It is food, not a supplement. Its only advantages are convenience, cost per gram, and a very high protein-to-calorie ratio — which makes it genuinely useful when you're cutting and struggling to fit protein into the calorie budget.

It has no magic. Whole-food protein produces the same result. Anyone who tells you that you *need* it to build muscle is selling it.

For vegetarians it's more useful than for others, because plant protein sources come attached to more carbohydrate and fat, and hitting 140 g of protein on a vegetarian diet without any powder means a lot of calories from dal and paneer. One or two scoops a day closes that gap cleanly.

## The plant-protein caveat, and how much it matters

Plant proteins are individually incomplete — most cereals are low in lysine, most legumes low in methionine — and have somewhat lower digestibility and a lower leucine content than animal proteins.

The fix is trivial and probably already in your diet: **pair a grain with a legume.** Rice and dal. Roti and chana. Idli and sambar. Rajma chawal. The Indian plate solved this problem several thousand years before anyone knew what an amino acid was.

The practical adjustment for a fully plant-based diet: aim for the **top of the protein range rather than the bottom** — 2.0–2.2 g/kg rather than 1.6 — and make sure a decent share comes from soya, which is the one plant protein with a complete profile and animal-comparable digestibility.

## What to do when you can't hit it

In order of preference:

1. Add a protein source to a meal that currently has none. Most people have at least one — the breakfast that's toast and tea, the snack that's biscuits.
2. Replace a carbohydrate snack with a protein one. Yoghurt instead of a packet of something.
3. Add a shake.
4. Accept 1.4 g/kg for a while. It is not optimal. It is 85% of the benefit, and it beats the plan you abandon because it was too demanding.
