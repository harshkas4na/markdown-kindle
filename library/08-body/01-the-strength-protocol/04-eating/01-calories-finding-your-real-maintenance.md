# Calories: Finding Your Real Maintenance

Maintenance is the number of calories at which your bodyweight is stable over time. Every nutrition decision you make is expressed relative to it. You cannot get this from a calculator, but a calculator is a fine place to start guessing.

## Step 1 — The estimate

Mifflin-St Jeor, which is the best-validated of the common equations.

**Men:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
**Women:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Then multiply by an activity factor to get your total daily energy expenditure:

| Factor | Your life |
|---|---|
| 1.2 | Desk job, no training, under 5,000 steps |
| 1.375 | Desk job, training 3× a week, ~7,000 steps |
| 1.55 | Training 4–5× a week, on your feet some of the day, ~10,000 steps |
| 1.725 | Training 6× a week or physically demanding job |
| 1.9 | Both |

**Worked example.** 24-year-old man, 70 kg, 175 cm, desk job, lifting three times a week:

- BMR = 700 + 1,094 − 120 + 5 = **1,679**
- TDEE = 1,679 × 1.375 = **≈ 2,310 kcal**

## Step 2 — Ignore the estimate

Not entirely, but understand what it is: a population average with an error bar of roughly ±300 kcal for any given individual. Three hundred calories is the entire size of a normal surplus or deficit. So the calculator can be wrong by more than the intervention.

Where does the error come from? Mostly **NEAT** — the calories you burn moving without exercising. Fidgeting, pacing, gesturing, standing up, taking stairs. It varies between individuals by many hundreds of calories a day, it's largely unconscious, and no equation captures it. It is the actual physiological content of "she has a fast metabolism" and "I gain weight just looking at food," and both of those statements are usually more true than the people saying them are given credit for.

## Step 3 — Find your real number

Two weeks. This is the only method that works.

1. Eat roughly your calculated TDEE. Track everything, honestly.
2. Weigh yourself every morning, same conditions.
3. At the end, average week 1's weights and average week 2's weights. Compare the two averages.

Then:

| Two-week trend | What it means | Adjust by |
|---|---|---|
| Weight stable (±0.3 kg) | You found maintenance | Nothing — this is your number |
| Up 0.5 kg or more | You're in a surplus | Subtract 250 kcal, repeat |
| Down 0.5 kg or more | You're in a deficit | Add 250 kcal, repeat |

**Use weekly averages, never single-day readings.** Daily bodyweight swings 1–2 kg on water, sodium, glycogen and digestive contents. A single morning weigh-in contains almost no information about fat mass; a seven-day average contains quite a lot.

## Step 4 — Accept that it moves

Maintenance is not a constant. It drifts down as you lose weight (less mass to move, and NEAT drops unconsciously in a deficit) and drifts up as you gain. Re-check it every eight to twelve weeks by the same method.

## Tracking, and how long to do it

Track everything, weighed on a kitchen scale, for **at least four weeks**. Not forever — the point is calibration, not permanent surveillance.

Four weeks of weighing food teaches you what 30 g of nuts actually looks like versus what you thought it looked like, what a restaurant portion really costs, and where your calories are actually coming from. After that you can mostly eyeball it, with a re-calibration week whenever progress stalls.

**The most reliable finding in this entire area** is that self-reported intake is wrong, in a consistent direction. People eating in a surplus under-report. People struggling to gain weight over-report — the skinny person who insists they "eat a ton" and tracks it honestly for the first time almost always discovers they were eating well under maintenance. Under-reporting errors of 20–40% are ordinary and are not lying; they're the oil in the pan, the second helping, the handful of something at 4pm, the weekend that doesn't get counted.

You are not exempt from this. Nobody is. Weigh it for four weeks.

## The things that break your tracking

In rough order of how much damage they do:

- **Cooking oil and ghee.** A tablespoon is ~120 kcal. Three tablespoons across a day of home cooking is a meaningful fraction of a surplus, and almost nobody counts it.
- **Liquid calories.** Juice, sweetened tea and coffee, soft drinks, alcohol. High calorie, near-zero satiety.
- **Weekends.** Five days of a 500 kcal deficit plus two days of a 1,250 kcal surplus is a net zero week. This is the single most common reason a diet "stops working."
- **Restaurant food.** Restaurant portions routinely carry 1.5–2× the calories of the same dish cooked at home, mostly in fat.
- **Bites, licks and tastes while cooking.** Individually trivial, collectively a few hundred calories a day.

## The one shortcut

If tracking is genuinely not something you'll sustain, there's a lower-fidelity method that still works: **fix your protein target, fix your meal structure, and adjust portion sizes based on the weekly weight trend.** You lose precision and you keep the feedback loop, which is the part that matters. It's worse than tracking. It's much better than nothing.
