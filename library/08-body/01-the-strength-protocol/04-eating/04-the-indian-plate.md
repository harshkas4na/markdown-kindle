# The Indian Plate

Almost every fitness resource is written around chicken breast, oats and cottage cheese, and then people who eat roti and dal conclude their food is the obstacle. It isn't. The Indian plate has two genuine weak points and both are fixable without abandoning it.

## The two real problems

**Problem 1: protein density is low.** A large, satisfying, entirely normal Indian meal — two rotis, a bowl of dal, sabzi, rice, curd — comes to roughly 600 kcal and about **18 g of protein**. Three of those meals is 1,800 kcal and 54 g of protein. For a 70 kg person needing 112 g minimum, that's less than half, on a full day's food.

**Problem 2: hidden fat.** Ghee, oil for tempering, oil in the sabzi, oil in the paratha. A tablespoon is ~120 kcal and home cooking routinely uses three or four across a day without anyone counting them. This is the single largest source of tracking error on this diet.

Everything else about the plate is fine. The grain-plus-legume pairing that Indian food is built on solves the amino-acid completeness problem automatically. The fibre content is high, which helps enormously on a cut. The vegetable variety is good.

## Fixing protein density

Six moves, in order of effort-to-payoff:

| Move | Protein added |
|---|---|
| Add 2 eggs or 100 g paneer to breakfast | +12 to +18 g |
| Swap regular curd for hung curd or Greek yoghurt, 150 g | +8 to +12 g |
| Add 50 g dry soya chunks to any sabzi or curry | +26 g |
| Double the dal portion | +7 to +14 g |
| One whey scoop post-training | +24 g |
| Replace an evening snack with roasted chana or sprouts | +10 to +15 g |

**Soya chunks are the highest-leverage item on this list and almost nobody uses them.** 52 g of protein per 100 g dry, cheap, shelf-stable, and they take on the flavour of whatever you cook them in. 50 g dry (which rehydrates to a substantial portion) adds 26 g of protein for about 170 kcal. Nothing else in the vegetarian world comes close on that ratio.

## A 2,600 kcal vegetarian bulking day, 140 g protein

For a 70 kg lifter. This is a template to modify, not a prescription.

| Meal | Food | kcal | Protein |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | 3 eggs (or 120 g paneer), 2 rotis, 250 ml milk | 640 | 31 g |
| Mid-morning | 40 g peanuts, 1 banana | 330 | 11 g |
| Lunch | 1.5 cups rice, 1.5 cups dal, sabzi with 50 g soya, curd | 900 | 48 g |
| Post-training | 1 whey scoop in milk | 270 | 32 g |
| Dinner | 3 rotis, 100 g paneer sabzi, salad | 620 | 26 g |
| **Total** | | **2,760** | **148 g** |

To make it a cutting day at ~2,000 kcal: drop the mid-morning nuts, halve the rice, cut cooking oil to one tablespoon total, keep everything else. Protein stays near 130 g, calories land around 2,000. **Cut carbs and fat; never cut protein.** That's the whole principle of adjusting any plan up or down.

## Eating out and street food

You don't have to stop. Three rules cover most of the damage:

- **Assume 1.5× what you'd estimate.** Restaurant food carries more oil, butter and cream than home food. Estimating high is more accurate than estimating honestly.
- **Choose tandoori and grilled over gravy.** The gravy is where the cream, cashew paste and oil live. Tandoori chicken, seekh kebab, grilled paneer are all genuinely good choices.
- **Pre-budget it.** If you know Saturday dinner is out, eat lighter earlier that day. Planned excess doesn't derail anything; unplanned excess repeated weekly is what kills a diet.

## The vegetarian bottom line

A vegetarian Indian diet supports serious muscle building without difficulty. The one adjustment worth making, relative to what this book says elsewhere: aim for the **top of the protein range — 2.0–2.2 g/kg — rather than the bottom**, to account for lower digestibility and leucine content in plant sources, and make sure a meaningful share comes from soya, dairy or whey rather than exclusively from dal and grains.

That's it. That's the whole adjustment. Everything else in this book applies unchanged.
