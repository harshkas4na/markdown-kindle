# Carbs, Fat, and Everything Else

Protein has a number. Calories have a direction. Everything remaining in nutrition is preference, satiety management, and small effects — which is good news, because it means the part of your diet that's culturally and personally yours can stay yours.

## Setting fat and carbs

Order of operations: calories first, protein second, then fat, then carbs get whatever's left.

**Fat: a floor, not a target.** Set it at **0.5–1.0 g per kg of bodyweight**, minimum around 20% of total calories. Below that, hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption start to suffer, and food gets unpleasant. Above that is fine if you prefer it.

**Carbs: the remainder.** Take your calorie target, subtract protein calories (4 per gram) and fat calories (9 per gram), divide what's left by 4.

**Worked example.** 70 kg man, 2,600 kcal bulking target:

- Protein: 140 g × 4 = 560 kcal
- Fat: 70 g × 9 = 630 kcal
- Remaining: 2,600 − 1,190 = 1,410 kcal ÷ 4 = **352 g carbs**

## The carbs-versus-fat question

Settled, and duller than the argument around it suggests: **when calories and protein are matched, the carb-to-fat ratio has little effect on body composition.** Low-carb works. High-carb works. Keto works. They all work because they all produce a calorie deficit, and they all stop working when they don't.

So choose on three criteria that actually vary between people:

- **Satiety.** Which macro keeps *you* full? This is individual and it's the most important input.
- **Training performance.** Carbohydrate is the primary fuel for hard resistance training. Very low-carb diets often cost you reps on the last sets, and reps are the progression. If your numbers drop when carbs drop, that's your answer.
- **Sustainability.** The best diet is the one you'll still be on in six months.

## Fibre

25–35 g a day, and most people miss it badly. It's the most under-rated variable in adherence: fibre is what makes a deficit tolerable, because high-fibre foods are bulky and slow. A 1,800 kcal day of dal, vegetables, roti and fruit feels completely different from a 1,800 kcal day of white rice and oil.

Sources: whole pulses, vegetables, fruit with the skin, oats, whole grains.

## Water

3–4 litres a day, more in heat or when training hard. This isn't a wellness platitude — mild dehydration measurably reduces strength output, and it makes your morning weigh-ins noisier, which corrupts the one measurement you're using to steer.

## Micronutrients

If you're eating a varied diet with vegetables and fruit, you're mostly fine. Three worth a specific thought:

- **Vitamin D.** Widespread deficiency, including in sunny countries, because most people work indoors. Worth an actual blood test rather than a guess. Supplement if low.
- **Iron.** Relevant for menstruating women and for vegetarians. Plant iron is poorly absorbed; vitamin C alongside the meal helps considerably.
- **B12.** Not present in plant foods. If you're vegan, supplement it. Not optional.

## Meal timing, honestly

- **Meal frequency doesn't matter** for body composition. Three meals, six meals, two meals — matched calories and protein produce matched results. Pick what fits your day.
- **Breakfast is not mandatory** and skipping it is not metabolically harmful. It's a schedule question.
- **Eating at night does not make you fat.** Calories don't have a clock. What's true is that late-night eating is often *unplanned* eating, and unplanned eating is where the surplus comes from — the mechanism is behavioural, not metabolic.
- **Intermittent fasting** is a calorie-control tool that works well for some people and badly for others. It has no independent fat-loss magic. If eating in a window helps you eat less, use it; if it makes you ravenous at 8pm, don't.
- **The post-workout anabolic window** is not 30 minutes. Protein within a few hours either side of training is sufficient.

## Alcohol

Seven calories per gram, no nutritional contribution, and it suppresses muscle protein synthesis and disrupts sleep architecture for the night. A heavy night costs you the calories, the next day's training quality, and the recovery.

That's the honest accounting. It's not a prohibition — it's information for deciding what a night out costs and whether you want to pay it.

## Diet breaks

On any cut running longer than about twelve weeks, take a **one to two week break at maintenance calories**. Not a break from tracking, not a free-for-all — an intentional period of eating at maintenance.

It helps in two ways: it partially reverses the adaptive drop in NEAT and hunger hormones that accumulates during a long deficit, and it's psychologically restorative in a way that makes the next block possible. People who diet-break tend to finish long cuts; people who grind straight through tend to abandon them at week fourteen and regain everything.

## The one rule that replaces all of this

If you throw the chapter away, keep this: **eat mostly whole, minimally processed food, hit your protein, and control the total.** Everything above is optimisation on top of a foundation that is genuinely that simple, and the optimisation is worth maybe 10% of your result.
