# Bulking, Cutting, Recomping

Your Pass 2 classification gave you one of these. This chapter gives you the rate rules — the numbers that separate a bulk from getting fat and a cut from losing the muscle you spent a year building.

## The rate rules

| Phase | Surplus / deficit | Weekly weight change | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lean bulk** | +10–15% over maintenance | +0.25% to +0.5% of bodyweight | 4–6 months |
| **Slow bulk (advanced)** | +5–10% | +0.25% of bodyweight | 6+ months |
| **Moderate cut** | −20% below maintenance | −0.5% to −0.75% of bodyweight | 8–16 weeks |
| **Aggressive cut** | −25–30% | −1% of bodyweight | Under 8 weeks, only from high body fat |
| **Recomp** | maintenance ± 5% | ~0, with waist falling | 4–6 months |

For a 70 kg person: a lean bulk is **+0.18 to +0.35 kg per week**, a moderate cut is **−0.35 to −0.5 kg per week**. Those numbers are much smaller than most people expect, and that is the point of the chapter.

## Why bulking slowly is not just aesthetic caution

Because of the ceiling from Part 2. Your maximum muscle gain in the best possible year is around 9 kg — about 0.75 kg a month, or roughly 0.17 kg a week, and that's the *upper bound* for a first-year trainee.

So if you're gaining 1 kg a week, at most a sixth of that is muscle. You are gaining fat at five times the rate you're gaining muscle, and every kilo of it has to come off later in a cut that will cost you months and some of the muscle too.

The data on gain rates supports this directly: gaining at roughly 0.24–0.38% of bodyweight per week produces something like **70–75% of the gain as lean mass**. Faster than that and the fat fraction climbs steeply. Eric Helms recommends advanced lifters cap it at 0.25% per week, precisely because the muscle-gain ceiling is so low at that stage that anything faster is pure fat.

**The dirty-bulk mistake**, stated plainly: gaining 15 kg over a winter, of which maybe 4 kg is muscle, then cutting 11 kg over four months and losing 1 kg of the muscle on the way. Net after eight months: +3 kg muscle, and a miserable time. The same eight months at a proper lean bulk rate: +5 kg muscle, no cut needed, and you looked reasonable throughout.

## Why cutting slowly matters too

The mechanism is different. In a deficit, the body will draw energy from both fat and lean tissue, and the ratio depends on how large the deficit is, how much protein you eat, and whether you're still training with meaningful loads.

Keep three things and you keep almost all your muscle through a cut:

1. **Protein at the top of the range** — 2.0–2.2 g/kg.
2. **Keep training heavy.** Do not switch to "toning" — high reps, light weights, circuits. The signal that tells your body to keep the muscle is *load*. Maintain your working weights even as volume drops.
3. **Deficit under 25%.** Larger deficits accelerate lean mass loss without proportionally accelerating fat loss.

The most common cutting error is doing all three wrong at once — cutting protein because "carbs and protein are both calories," dropping the weights and adding cardio, and slashing calories by 40% out of impatience. That reliably produces a smaller version of the same body, which is precisely what nobody wants.

## Recomping

Losing fat and building muscle simultaneously. It genuinely works, and it works best for specific people:

- **Untrained beginners.** Resistance training is such a novel stimulus that muscle grows even without a surplus.
- **People with higher body fat.** The stored energy substitutes for a dietary surplus; an overweight beginner can gain muscle in a meaningful deficit.
- **Returning lifters.** Muscle memory makes regaining previously-held muscle far faster than building it the first time.

For lean, trained lifters, recomping is slow and marginal — possible under near-perfect conditions, but for most people past the beginner stage, separate bulk and cut phases produce more in the same time.

**How to run one:** eat at maintenance or a very small deficit, protein at 2.0–2.2 g/kg, train hard and progressively. Then — and this is the part that matters — **judge it by the tape and the photos, never by the scale.** The scale will barely move for months. That's what success looks like in a recomp, and people who don't know that quit at week six convinced it failed.

## Choosing your phase length

**Minimum twelve weeks.** Below that you cannot separate signal from noise: water weight, glycogen, food volume in the gut and hormonal fluctuation all move the scale more than twelve weeks of real change in a slow-bulking lifter.

The most damaging pattern in this entire book is not choosing wrong. It's oscillating — three weeks bulking, then a photo looks soft, then three weeks cutting, then feeling small, then bulking again. That person spends a year at exactly the same bodyweight and body fat, works hard the whole time, and concludes their body is resistant to change.

Pick a direction. Write the end date. Do not re-evaluate before it.

## Adjusting mid-phase

Check the weekly average every two weeks against your target rate.

| Observation | Action |
|---|---|
| Bulking, gaining too fast | Cut 200 kcal |
| Bulking, no gain in 2 weeks | Add 200 kcal |
| Cutting, losing too fast | Add 200 kcal — you're leaving muscle behind |
| Cutting, no loss in 2 weeks | Cut 200 kcal, and check your tracking honesty first |
| Recomping, waist up and weight up | Small deficit |
| Recomping, weight down and lifts falling | Add 200 kcal |

Adjust in 200 kcal steps, no more often than every two weeks. Adjusting weekly means reacting to noise, and reacting to noise looks exactly like having no plan.

## When to stop a bulk

Any of: you've hit 18–20% body fat (men) or 28–30% (women); your waist has grown faster than your other measurements for six weeks; the surplus stops producing strength gains and only produces weight. That last one is the useful signal — a bulk that isn't buying you strength is just buying you fat.

## When to stop a cut

Any of: you've reached your target; you've been cutting sixteen weeks (take a diet break at maintenance regardless of whether you're done); your strength has been falling for three weeks straight; sleep, mood or menstrual function is disrupted. The last one is not negotiable.
