# Your Ceiling and Your Rate

Two questions this chapter answers: how much muscle can you eventually carry, and how fast can it arrive. Both have defensible estimates. Both are routinely lied about, in both directions — by supplement marketing that inflates them and by discouraged people who deflate them.

## The rate: what a year actually buys

The most-cited model comes from Lyle McDonald. For a natural man training and eating well:

| Training year | Muscle gained (upper bound) | Per month |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | ~9 kg (20 lb) | ~0.75 kg |
| Year 2 | ~4.5 kg (10 lb) | ~0.4 kg |
| Year 3 | ~2.3 kg (5 lb) | ~0.2 kg |
| Year 4+ | ~1 kg (2 lb) | barely measurable |

Women gain at roughly half these rates in absolute terms — which, given roughly half the starting lean mass, is a similar proportional gain, not a lesser one.

**Read those as upper bounds under near-optimal conditions**, not expectations. Optimal means consistent training, protein at target, adequate sleep, appropriate surplus, no interruptions. Most people, most of the time, land at 3–6 kg in year one rather than 9. The gap is not genetics. The gap is missed weeks, under-eaten protein, and programs that were never progressive.

Three things fall out of this table and each one is worth sitting with.

**Year one is more than years two, three, four and five combined.** That is the entire argument for not spending your first year experimenting with exotic protocols. Do something reasonable, do it every week, do not miss the window.

**The scale moves faster than muscle does.** ~0.75 kg of muscle a month is the ceiling; if the scale is going up 2 kg a month, at least 1.25 kg of that is fat and water. This is why Part 4's rate rules exist.

**Nobody's transformation photos are one year apart** unless something else was involved. When the timeline in a before-and-after seems impossible against this table, it usually is.

## The ceiling: Casey Butt's model

Casey Butt analysed drug-tested top-level natural bodybuilders and derived a prediction of maximum lean body mass from height, wrist and ankle. In imperial units:

```
Max LBM (lb, at ~5% body fat)
  = height(in)^1.5 × ( √wrist(in) / 22.667  +  √ankle(in) / 17.0104 )
```

To use it: divide your cm measurements by 2.54 to get inches, run the formula, then divide the result by 2.205 to get kilos. To find your maximum *bodyweight* at a given body fat, divide the lean mass by (1 − BF%). At 10% body fat that means dividing by 0.9.

**Worked example.** 175 cm (68.9 in), wrist 17.5 cm (6.89 in), ankle 22.5 cm (8.86 in):

- 68.9^1.5 = 572
- √6.89 ÷ 22.667 = 0.116
- √8.86 ÷ 17.010 = 0.175
- 572 × 0.291 = **166 lb lean = 75 kg**
- At 10% body fat: 75 ÷ 0.9 = **83 kg bodyweight**

That is a genuinely large, lean 175 cm man. It is also a ceiling that takes most people the better part of a decade to approach and that most people never reach, because reaching it requires near-perfect execution for years.

Butt's model is more conservative than McDonald's or Alan Aragon's, and it's the one to prefer precisely because it's built from measured competitors rather than from a rule of thumb. He also published proportional targets from the same dataset — chest ≈ 6.5 × wrist, flexed arm ≈ 2.34 × wrist, calf ≈ 2.21 × wrist — which are useful mainly as a reality check on internet arm-size claims.

## The sanity check: FFMI

Fat-free mass index is lean mass in kg divided by height in metres squared. It's a quick way to place yourself.

```
FFMI = lean mass (kg) ÷ height(m)²
```

| FFMI (men) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 18–19 | Untrained average |
| 20–21 | Visibly trained, a year or two in |
| 22–23 | Clearly athletic, several years of good training |
| 24–25 | Near the natural ceiling |
| 26+ | Very rare naturally; usually indicates drugs or a measurement error |

Women's scale runs roughly 3 points lower at each tier. The 175 cm example above works out to 75 ÷ 1.75² = FFMI 24.6 — consistent with "near the natural ceiling," which is what the Butt formula claimed it was.

## What to actually do with these numbers

Three things, and only three.

**Set a five-year expectation, not a three-month one.** If your ceiling is 83 kg at 10% and you're at 65 kg at 15%, you now know the size of the project. It's years. Knowing that upfront is protective — it stops week nine from feeling like failure.

**Set an intermediate target.** Take your ceiling, subtract 30%, and aim there. That's a target you can plausibly reach in two to three years of good training and it will already put you well outside the normal range.

**Then stop thinking about it.** The ceiling is a horizon, not a plan. The plan is one more rep than last week. Nobody has ever reached their genetic potential by calculating it more precisely; every single person who got close did it by adding weight to a bar for a very long time.

> A genuine finding buried in the somatotype research is worth repeating here: individuals with quite different starting morphologies achieve similar hypertrophy outcomes when the training stimulus is appropriate. The frame sets where you finish. It does not set whether you improve.
