# The Body-Type Question, Answered Honestly

You want to know what body type you are. This chapter tells you why the standard answer is worthless, and what the real answer looks like — because there *is* a real answer, it's just not the one on the poster in your gym.

## Where ectomorph, mesomorph and endomorph came from

In the 1940s an American psychologist named William Sheldon proposed that human physiques sorted into three components — ectomorphy (linear, slight), mesomorphy (muscular, square), endomorphy (round, soft) — and, crucially, that these predicted *personality*. Ectomorphs were nervous and cerebral, mesomorphs assertive and adventurous, endomorphs relaxed and sociable.

The personality half of the theory collapsed under scrutiny and was largely abandoned by the 1950s. It was built on a photographic dataset collected in ways that would not survive an ethics board today, and it hardened cultural prejudice — the lazy fat man, the dominant athlete — into something that looked like science.

The body-shape half survived, mostly because it's descriptively useful. Sports scientists still use somatotype scores to describe athlete populations: a scoping review of elite athletes across many sports finds that, unsurprisingly, sprinters and marathoners have different average physiques. That is a real and mildly interesting finding about groups.

## Why it is useless as a training instruction

Three reasons, and each one is fatal on its own.

**It's a continuum, not three boxes.** Basal metabolic rate, muscle fibre type distribution, bone density, limb proportions, appetite regulation — every one of these varies smoothly across the population. There is no boundary where ectomorph stops and mesomorph starts. Real somatotyping doesn't even claim otherwise; it gives you three scores out of seven, like 3-5-2. Everybody is a blend. The three-box version is the folk-science compression of it, and the compression is where the information dies.

**It predicts nothing about what you should do.** There is no body of evidence linking somatotype to which training or eating strategy works better. Nobody has run the study where you sort people into three types, give each type its "matched" protocol, and find better outcomes. The endomorph-should-do-more-cardio, ectomorph-should-lift-heavy-and-low-rep advice is invented. It has the shape of a finding and none of the substance.

**It's describing your current state and calling it your nature.** This is the deepest problem. Take a photo of someone who has been sedentary for ten years and eating at a surplus: round midsection, soft, "endomorph." Now that person trains and eats well for eighteen months. They are now square-shouldered and lean: "mesomorph." Did their type change? Their skeleton didn't. Everything the chart looked at did.

Longitudinal training studies find that people with quite different starting morphologies reach similar hypertrophy outcomes when the training stimulus is appropriate. The type wasn't the constraint. The stimulus was.

## The real reason the myth is sticky

Because it *feels* true, and it feels true because it encodes one thing that is genuinely real: **people respond differently, and some of that difference is fixed.**

That part is not a myth. Your skeleton is fixed. Your limb-to-torso proportions are fixed and they genuinely change which lifts feel good. Your appetite set point is largely fixed and it genuinely makes eating 3,500 calories trivial for one person and miserable for another. Your NEAT — the calories you burn fidgeting, pacing, gesturing, not sitting still — varies between individuals by hundreds of calories a day and is largely not under conscious control. Your recovery capacity is partly genetic.

So the *intuition* behind body types is sound. Two people do the same program and get different results, and it isn't always effort.

The error is in what follows. The myth says: *look at yourself, name the type, apply the matched protocol.* But you cannot see appetite regulation in a mirror. You cannot see NEAT in a mirror. You cannot see fibre type or recovery capacity in a mirror. Every variable that actually matters is invisible to the method the myth uses.

## What replaces it

Split "body type" into two questions that are answerable, and answer them by different methods.

**What is fixed about me?** Answer with a tape measure. Your skeletal frame — wrist and ankle circumference relative to height — does not change with training and puts a soft ceiling on how much muscle you can eventually carry. That's Chapter 2 and Chapter 4 of this part. It's the only part of "type" you can know on day one.

**How do I respond?** Answer with a logbook, over eight to twelve weeks. Appetite, recovery, volume tolerance, whether you gain strength faster than size, where you store fat first, how your joints handle frequency. Every one of these is a real, stable, individual trait. Not one of them is visible before you train. Chapter 5 gives you the profile sheet.

And in between those two sits the thing that actually determines what you do next month, which is neither fixed nor discovered but simply *current*: your body fat and your training age. That's Chapter 3, and it's the chart that produces an actual instruction.

> **The reframe:** you don't have a body type. You have a skeleton you can measure today, a current state you can measure today, and a response profile you can only learn by training. The first is destiny-ish, the second is a decision, the third is homework.

Go get a tape measure.
