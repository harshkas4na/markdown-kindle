# How each founder hears a number

> [!STORY] Four words that stopped a meeting
> In the 31 August standup, with several people in the room, Alok said: "Hold on, take a step back. What is precision recall basically means?" Then, a little later: "All I'm trying to say is that get the definition very precise, because I was kind of getting confused."
> He was not confused. He was refusing to continue on a metric nobody had defined. A little later still: "Presenting recall for a small set would be misleading, very misleading."
> The same bad number, shown to three founders, gets three different reactions. This chapter is a guide to each.

## Alok: the definition and the denominator, before the number

A recall of 100 percent on four hand-picked accounts got no credit at all. A qualified figure with its target set stated will land. A good number on a subset gets taken apart in one sentence, in front of everyone. His standing instruction to anyone blocked: "anytime you are blocked on something, try to avoid it and go for a solution."

## Preetam: the method before the result

"First of all, you have to set up the experiment in the proper way... unless that's correct then the rest of it doesn't really matter."

He will not discuss a result until the experiment is right. He also fixed the format for anything customer-facing: **Problem, Cause, How it was fixed, If not completely, what is pending and ETA.** Four headings. Use them. And **read his design doc before asking him the question it answers.** It is the cheapest way to be worth his time.

## Arun: usage over shipping, and who the work is for

"It's easy for me to go to a customer and say I will build you these agents... but if they are not clicking on it, if they are not using it... we need to build what is usable."

"If everything you are doing is to benefit this one person and nobody else knows about it, it's not good for us."

Bring him adoption numbers, not completed tickets. In writing he is structured: the constraint comes before the task. Mirror that shape when reporting back: the risk you saw, then what you did.

## The same bad number, three ways

Say the number is bad. Say it to each of them like this.

- **To Alok:** here is the target set, here is the denominator, here is the number, and it is bad.
- **To Preetam:** here is how the experiment was set up, here is why I trust it, here is the number.
- **To Arun:** here is what this means for whether the customer uses it, and here is what I would do next.

## The thing to fix about how you show up

It was graded twice across the four interview rounds: you answer adjacent instead of head-on, and you undersell a real thing with a hedge. Asked whether you wrote smart contracts, you described the architecture without saying yes or no. Asked how good your SQL is, you called catching a bug in Claude-generated SQL "that's how much I got the idea."

**First sentence commits. The detail comes after.** In a room with Alok in it, that is not a style note. It is the difference between a number that lands and a number that gets taken apart.

## The short-message rule

Drafts for colleagues are two to four plain lines. The detail stays in the repo and the reader asks if they want more. In writing for anything you send or submit: no em dashes, none of the banned words ("leverage", "passionate", "thrilled", "seamless", "proven track record"). It should read like you typed it at 1am, because you did.

## Traps

- Opening with the detail and committing last.
- Quoting a number before the denominator.
- Defending a number instead of showing how it was set up.
